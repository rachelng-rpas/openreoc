# BVLOS Operations — Lessons Learnt (12 July – 4 August 2026)

## Pre-Flight Planning & Risk Assessment
- Night surveillance/compliance operations are a distinct planning case: the mission is client-directed in real time (via shared screen / video call) rather than a pre-loaded autonomous route, so plan for **manual takeoff and live tasking** rather than a fixed flight plan. Launch workflow: Team → Dock → virtual cockpit → Takeoff.
- Range extension isn't free endurance. The task area is currently ~7.5 km radius from the hub (≈80% of the drone's rated capability, per the regulator's 80% rule). Proposals to push toward ~9.6 km must be weighed against battery reality — much of the extra range is spent just transiting out, arriving with little margin before RTH triggers. Extra range on paper ≠ extra useful time on task.
- Verify client-supplied flight routes against the actual operating area and confirm them with stakeholders before loading into FH2. Group and cluster nearby routes so pilots aren't re-checking and re-selecting each one — this reduces setup load and selection error.
- Treat firmware as a controlled change. An un-updated FH2 firmware state left drones sitting in dock at **0% on hub open** (remote debug OFF, power-saver ON — a DJI-side behaviour). Don't push firmware updates without checking and flight-testing with the software team first, and roll out to **partial hubs only**, so a bad update can't take every hub down at once.
- Confirm the drone is correctly re-paired with its dock from the controller **before** opening the dock or attempting a test flight — power the drone ON with the dock **CLOSED** to verify pairing first.
- Build site activity into the plan. Maintenance visits, solar-panel cleaning, dongle/hardware updates and other ground work must be known and de-conflicted against flight and, critically, landing windows. Where possible, issue a NOTAM/site notice and brief site personnel in advance.

## Airmanship & Flight Technique
- For night object identification, **Thermal in White Hot** was the most effective mode for picking objects out of clutter (boat vs whale vs reef vs wave return) — but the visual picture while flying still isn't ideal, so combine sensors rather than relying on one.
- Spotlight is highly effective for detail: ~100–200 m standoff with the spotlight gives good detail on a vessel; ~50 m gives a very clear picture (≈100 m AGL). Close the range deliberately once a target is identified.
- Watch for control-input conflicts: gimbal control and spotlight brightness both use the keyboard arrows, so gimbal movement can inadvertently change spotlight brightness — be aware of the shared binding and move deliberately.
- Use thermal + RGB together with the spotlight to capture evidentiary detail (e.g. vessel registration numbers) — one sensor confirms the other.
- Obstacle-avoidance sensors can interfere with return-to-base, throttling the aircraft to under 2 m/s. Flying the drone **sideways** to keep the obstacle out of the sensor's primary field let it recover and land safely — know this workaround before it costs you battery.
- Strong/gusting wind on the return leg drains battery far faster than benign conditions — don't push a run to completion when the wind is against you coming home (see Case Study 3). A tailwind out becomes a costly headwind back.

## Airspace Coordination & Traffic Deconfliction
- Establishing positive communication with manned traffic does **not** guarantee they'll stay clear: an aircraft acknowledged coordination with "Copy" and then still crossed into the operating area (see Case Study 1). Plan for the possibility that agreed separation won't be honoured.
- When a manned aircraft's trajectory is unpredictable, holding a **stable low hover** can be safer than initiating RTH — an automated RTH climb/track into an uncertain flight path can *introduce* risk rather than reduce it. Increase separation by descending and holding, then let the traffic resolve itself.
- Descend proactively to open vertical separation the moment traffic enters the area — dropping from ~100 m to ~70–80 m AGL bought roughly 1,000 ft vertical / 1,000 m horizontal separation in one encounter.
- A radio call sharing your position, height and duration may go **unanswered** — don't assume the manned aircraft has heard or will respond. Act on what you *see* on ADS-B / FlightRadar, not on an expected acknowledgement.
- Detect early with multiple sources: manned traffic was picked up on ADS-B and FlightRadar (one within the 3 nm boundary, one entering at ~2,000 ft AMSL while the drone worked at 100 m AGL) — the electronic picture is what gave time to react.

## Communications (CRM)
- Livestreamed, client-directed operations raise the importance of **on-call / standby tech support**, especially outside standard hours — with a client tasking the pilot live, backup coverage matters more than for a routine autonomous mission. Arrange standby cover explicitly for out-of-hours live ops.
- Landing-zone coordination needs a reliable comms path to the ground. With no speaker or direct means to warn site personnel, crews were reduced to phoning a site contact (sometimes unanswered) to clear the pad. Establish and **test** a ground-comms/escalation chain before operating over an active site.
- Brief site personnel on what to do when a drone is returning to land — several near-misses came from people staying in the landing zone simply because they didn't know to clear it.

## In-Flight Decision-Making
- Battery margin overrides run completion, especially in wind: pushing to finish a run with the wind against the return leg fried a battery on RTH and forced an off-dock emergency landing that couldn't be cancelled to re-enter the dock (see Case Study 3). Return with margin.
- Don't RTH into uncertainty. When nearby traffic is behaving unpredictably, a controlled hover with maximised separation can beat an automated RTH whose climb and track you don't fully command.
- If the landing pad isn't clear, hover and hold (battery permitting) while the obstruction is resolved rather than forcing a landing — but recognise the hover is spending margin, so escalate the clear-the-pad call **early**, not late.
- A drone **cannot land when two docks are open** — the landing system is confused by the visual cues/markings on both. If a second dock is open with no drone in it, expect the landing to fail; see Case Study 2 for the recovery procedure.

## Human Factors / Reflections
- **Tooling matured this period.** The wind predictor is now a self-contained single-file web app (Wind Predictor & Pilot Day Plan). It ingests the Daily Log export and forecasts, per hub and wind band, the number of flights, airborne time, ops duration, projected finish and 1:15 pm risk. A new **Pilot Day Plan** tab models a pilot's full day across two hubs, chaining the second hub's start off the first hub's projected finish plus a handover buffer. Pilot name is stored for reference but explicitly **excluded from every calculation** (stats are hub + wind band only). It runs entirely in-browser with no upload/persistence, matching the daily re-upload workflow.
- **New sites, new baselines.** Three new hubs were added to one client's network — each needs its own logged data before the predictor is reliable for it, so early forecasts for new hubs carry wide uncertainty until the sample builds.
- **Project ownership.** Took the lead on a new utility/powerline survey project — securing client approval, running stakeholder engagement across the site's different residences, sorting the operating area and routes with the client, then briefing pilots on the coming week's routes. A supporting route-planning/sortie tool was built to manage this (see the separate Sortie development log). Owning the stakeholder layer, not just the flying, is now part of the role.
- **Deliberate step-up on a novel op.** On the reef night operation, the first flight was flown under supervision and the second solo — stepping up autonomy on a new, high-tasking operation type rather than jumping straight to unsupervised.
- **Ground-state assumptions bite.** Drones found flat at 0% on hub open, personnel unexpectedly in the landing zone, and a tech opening a dock mid-RTH all trace to the same root: the ground state wasn't known or communicated before flight. Build "confirm ground state and who's on site" into both the pre-flight and pre-landing checks.

## Case Studies & Incident Reflections

### Case Study 1: Manned aircraft entering the operational area despite established coordination
**Scenario:** During a waypoint mapping mission at ~100 m AGL over a wind-farm survey site, a light training aircraft (Piper Cherokee) was observed via FlightRadar entering the operational area at ~2,000 ft AMSL. Positive radio communication was established, and the remote pilot advised that the drone would remain on one side of a highway boundary and below 400 ft AGL, then proactively descended the drone to ~70–80 m AGL (~900–1,000 ft AMSL) — achieving roughly 1,000 m horizontal and 1,000 ft vertical separation. The aircraft acknowledged with "Copy" — then continued across the boundary into the vicinity of the drone operation.

**Outcome:** Because the aircraft's path was unpredictable, the remote pilot held the drone in a **stable hover** at ~70–80 m AGL and made no further manoeuvres. Critically, the pilot chose *not* to trigger RTH, judging that an automated RTH climb/track could introduce further risk given the aircraft's uncertain trajectory. A few minutes later the aircraft broadcast a climb to overfly the airfield, cleared the area, and proceeded to circuits. The mission resumed once the threat had passed. The mid-mission pause consumed enough battery that an additional flight was needed to finish, with the aircraft returning on insufficient margin to complete in one go. No collision risk, loss of control, or damage occurred. An aircraft-proximity incident report was submitted.

**Contributing factors / observations:**
- Coordination was acknowledged but not honoured — "Copy" did not translate into the aircraft holding clear.
- A separate encounter the same period reinforced the pattern: a low-flying fixed-wing aircraft detected on ADS-B/FlightRadar within the 3 nm boundary (drone ~330 m, aircraft ~609 m) prompted the same response — take manual control, descend to hold ≥1,000 ft separation, hover until clear — and a radio call sharing position/height/duration went **unanswered**.

**Lessons:**
- Positive communication is not positive separation — plan for acknowledged coordination to fail, and keep an independent detection picture (ADS-B/FlightRadar) running throughout.
- When traffic is unpredictable, a controlled hover with maximised separation can be safer than an automated RTH whose climb and track you don't fully command.
- Proactive descent to open vertical separation is a strong first move — do it early, on detection, not once the conflict is close.
- Budget for the battery cost of a mid-mission avoidance hold: a traffic pause can turn a one-flight area into a two-flight one, so keep enough margin that a forced hover doesn't strand you.

### Case Study 2: Landing disrupted by uncoordinated site activity and a dual-dock-open confusion
**Scenario:** On a return-to-home with the aircraft only a few minutes out (battery ~28%), the crew noticed a technician at a trailer on site — a maintenance visit (updating drones with 4G dongles) that had **not** been notified in advance. Attempts to phone the site contact went unanswered. As the drone neared home, the technician opened one dock and removed the drone that was in it, leaving that dock open and empty. With the returning drone's own dock also open, **both docks were now open** — and the landing system, confused by the landing markings on two open docks (oriented in different directions, with only the central mast as a shared reference), could not identify the correct dock to land on. An earlier, separate event had the same root cause: a person cleaning a dock's solar panel remained in the landing zone as the drone returned, with no speaker or direct means to warn them off.

**Outcome:** After the crew confirmed the techs were clear and okay to land, RTH was paused ~30 m out and the recovery procedure below was used; the drone landed successfully with ~18% battery remaining and no damage. In the solar-panel case, the drone had just enough battery to hover while a phone call arranged for the zone to be cleared — a near-miss that could have ended in an off-dock landing.

**Recovery procedure that worked (two docks open):**
- Continue RTH and begin the descent, then **pause** the descent.
- Manually lower the drone to directly above the correct open dock, down to a minimum ~5.5 m (checking mast clearance).
- Re-initiate the RTH function — the drone rises ~3–4 m and should then lock onto the correct dock and land.
- *Alternate if it still won't land:* have a technician place the drone back in the dock and confirm all okay, **or** launch from the ground and wait until the other dock is closed before re-attempting the landing.

**Lessons:**
- The landing system cannot resolve two open docks — treat "second dock open and empty" as a landing hazard and close it before recovering an aircraft.
- Un-notified site activity is a recurring landing risk: maintenance, panel cleaning and hardware updates must be de-conflicted against flight/landing windows, with a NOTAM/site notice and a briefed, reachable ground contact.
- Site personnel need a standing instruction to clear the landing zone when a drone is returning — and crews need a reliable, tested way to signal them (the phone-a-contact chain failed when the contact didn't answer).
- Know the dual-dock recovery procedure *before* you need it — improvising it at 28% battery is exactly the wrong time to learn it.

### Case Study 3: Battery exhausted pushing a run in gusting wind → off-dock emergency landing
**Scenario:** A remote pilot pushed to finish a run despite deteriorating wind (steady ~8 m/s, gusting ~12 m/s). On the return-to-home leg, the wind drained the battery far faster than planned.

**Outcome:** The aircraft made it back over the dock, but the battery was low enough that the system committed to an emergency landing that **could not be cancelled** to re-enter the dock — resulting in a landing out of dock. The aircraft came down off its intended point with no margin to reposition.

**Lessons:**
- Wind on the return leg is a battery multiplier — the ~4%/km return rule of thumb assumes benign conditions; a headwind home can blow through that budget. Set the turn-back point conservatively when wind is up.
- Once an emergency/auto landing commits, you may lose the ability to cancel and re-dock — so the decision that matters is made *earlier*, at the go/no-go on pushing the run, not over the dock.
- Gusts matter as much as steady wind: 8 m/s gusting 12 m/s is a different aircraft to fly and land than a steady 8 m/s, and can flip or displace it on final descent.
- Completing the last portion of a run is never worth an off-dock, no-margin landing — bank the safe return and finish with a follow-up flight.
