# BVLOS Operations — Lessons Learnt (5 August – 22 November 2026)

## Pre-Flight Planning & Risk Assessment
- **Large-area mapping split across several plans.** For a large site (~8–9 km²) flown as several plans with big terrain elevation change, **increase the overlap between plans** — we moved from 1–2 flight lines of overlap to 2–3. Also **align the course angle across all the plans**. Adjacent plans flown at different course angles didn't overlap properly once the lines were extended, and left small triangular no-data gaps in the point cloud.
- **Split vs resume is a genuine team disagreement.** One view: don't split into several plans at all, fly one plan and use *resume mission* so no overlap is needed. The other: split, with extra overlap and a common course angle. Pilots each have their own way of designing and optimising mapping missions — settle on a team standard, and write down the reasoning (terrain change, battery, coverage) rather than leaving it to individual preference.
- **Parachute-equipped aircraft change the plan.** Where a client works to an aviation risk standard (e.g. BARS), there is no flying over or near people without the parachute system. Practical points on the system we used (check yours):
  - It must be **armed manually after takeoff and before landing** — put both steps in the checklist.
  - It only deploys above a minimum height (~34.7 m AGL).
  - Strong wind or abrupt control inputs (high bank angle) can trigger it. In strong wind the safer choice is to disarm it **and keep the aircraft away from people**.
  - Practical flight time dropped to roughly 24 minutes (weather dependent) with the parachute fitted, so re-check range and battery planning.
- **Wildlife is a site hazard.** An eagle attacked an aircraft during a hover. Some sites carry a higher bird threat than others — put bird activity in the site risk assessment and record sightings per site.

## Airmanship & Flight Technique
- **Hovering invites trouble at bird-prone sites.** An eagle attacked during a hover. At sites with known raptor activity, minimise time spent hovering and budget battery for avoiding action.
- **Low-battery return with people at the landing site.** When someone (e.g. a person cleaning solar panels) was at the hub during a low-battery RTH, the workable response was: stay low, move close to the alternate landing site near the home point, hover, keep monitoring, and get the ROC / client to clear the person. Take a screenshot as evidence. (This extends Case Study 2 in the previous period's entry.)
- **Practise manual exposure before you need it.** The camera aircraft we use didn't offer shutter priority, so aperture, ISO and shutter speed all have to be set deliberately in manual mode. Practising with a dedicated tool and mission challenges teaches how the camera behaves (verify against your own aircraft's modes).

## Communications (CRM)
- **A warning from the aircraft may not be understood.** In a live-tasked compliance operation, people on a vessel were warned to move away but waved at the camera without noticing the warning. Don't assume an aerial warning has been received — use a second channel (the client, the ground team).
- **Rapid response works.** From receiving a report to having an aircraft on scene took about 10 minutes — a clear example of the value of a hub-based, client-directed response.
- **Answer client questions with evidence, quickly.** When a client queried a gap in delivered imagery, the answer depended on how fast the team could show where every photo was taken (see Case Study 3 and the coverage checker below).

## In-Flight Decision-Making
- **Confidence is not margin.** Cancelling an RTH because the remaining waypoints looked achievable burned the reserve (see Case Study 1). If policy says no cancel-RTH, the policy is the margin.
- **A frozen control interface burns battery.** Losing the control page mid-transit costs time, and time is battery (see Case Study 2). Rehearse the recovery steps, and start the return earlier at long range.
- **Say no when you don't have the context.** If you haven't been inducted, don't know the procedure or can't see the situation, the correct answer is no — even when someone tells you "it's just like" something you're already cleared for (see Case Study 4).
- **Battery-policy points from these events:** raise the landing target from 15% to 20% for more room, and keep a firm no-cancel-RTH rule.

## Human Factors / Reflections
- **Pressure stacks.** In the battery event, three things arrived together: the platform going down, this being the last capture of the site, and the "just two more waypoints" feeling. Name the pressure out loud before making the call.
- **Report it, even when you want to hide it.** The first instinct after the 10% landing was to not report it. Reporting it to the CRP was the right call, and the report is what turns the event into learning. A reporting culture depends on people filing the uncomfortable ones.
- **How seriously safety is treated.** Reflection on how many layers sit around a single BVLOS operation: limits in time and geography, client and stakeholder engagement, airports, airfields, vessels, other pilots, arrival and departure times, and ongoing threat and risk management. It's a lot more than the flying.
- **Standardise the playbook.** Drafted a common playbook template and an SOP on how to write a site playbook, so every site is documented the same way. Redrafted the BVLOS start-of-day, subsequent-flight and post-flight checklists.
- **Safety management.** Started developing the safety management system (SMS) and the safety investigation process with the CRP, and took over development of an ROC management tool.

## Tooling & Analysis
- **Flight Photo Coverage Checker.** Drop in a photo folder or a data-platform zip and it plots every image on satellite imagery from the GPS in the files, flags missing capture numbers and unusually large gaps, keeps panoramas separate, and previews a photo on click. Nothing is uploaded anywhere. It went from a published page to a standalone HTML file to a Chrome/Edge extension. What used to take ~15 minutes of re-uploading ~250 images into FH2 now takes seconds, so pilots can check coverage **before** delivering.
  - Browser extensions must **bundle** their libraries rather than load them from the web.
  - Windows "Extract All" can nest folders and break an extension install — small packaging details matter when rolling a tool out to a team.
- **Marine-zone range estimator.** To judge whether a vessel is inside a restricted zone, the HUD's altitude (AGL) and gimbal pitch are enough for trigonometry: **horizontal distance = AGL ÷ tan(|gimbal pitch|)**, with an optional bearing to draw a line on the map. Field testing showed it can run **8–12% off**, growing with distance and shallower gimbal angles (a small pitch error is amplified at shallow angles; the formula also ignores any height difference between the aircraft's reference and the surface). Position it as a quick assist for ambiguous calls, not a replacement for visual confirmation against the zone boundaries.
  - What was cut to keep it fast mid-flight: GPS coordinate entry and a flight log.
  - Next: collect more test data and calibrate to reduce the error, then show a **confidence range** instead of a single number. Entering the drone's coordinates to project the scenario on a map is still undecided — it adds load, and the tool needs to stay a quick-response aid.
- **Feasibility analysis for a hub network over a large linear asset network (hundreds of thousands of poles).** The lessons carry over to any similar analysis:
  - **Prove the method by hand first.** Run the real spatial analysis in a script on the real data (density grids, greedy hub placement, mission batching) before building a reusable tool. This caught early mistakes cheaply — fixed grids badly misrepresent a linear network, and picking the top-density sites naively clusters every hub in one area.
  - **Use a viability gate.** Place hubs greedily by real marginal coverage, and **stop when no remaining site clears a minimum annual workload** — deliberately leaving the rest to manual crews rather than forcing coverage everywhere.
  - **Test against synthetic data with a known answer** before shipping, especially without a live browser to check in.
  - **Productise only after the logic is proven**; for 600k+ points use typed arrays, a web worker and a custom canvas map to stay dependency-light.
  - **Get a colleague to test it.** Theirs found a real HTML-nesting bug that hid a whole tab, an inconsistency between annualised and non-annualised numbers, and UI clutter.
  - The biggest wins came from testing before trusting, not from writing code faster.
- **Training tools.** Built a tool for FPV training and one for practising manual camera settings (aperture, ISO, shutter), each with mission challenges.
- See also the separate **Sortie development log** for the route-planning tool and its two main lessons (check the real file format; a tool that fails "successfully" is worse than an error).

## Case Studies & Incident Reflections

### Case Study 1: Cancelled RTH to finish a capture → landing at ~10%
**Scenario:** Late in a multi-panorama task, the aircraft was at about 25% after the second-last panorama, and the final panorama was very close to the dock. Company policy said not to cancel the RTH, but the remote pilot cancelled it, judging there was enough battery to finish the last shot and land safely. The platform had also gone down around midday, and this was the last capture for the site.

**Outcome:** The return sequence used more battery than expected and the aircraft landed at about 10%, well below the landing target. The pilot's first reaction was to not report it. After reflecting, they reported it to the CRP and submitted an incident report, acknowledging that the aircraft should have returned earlier.

**Contributing factors / observations:**
- Confidence in the battery estimate, rather than margin, drove the decision.
- Time pressure: a platform outage and the "last shot of the site" effect.
- A written policy (no cancel-RTH) existed and was set aside for what looked like a small gain.

**Lessons:**
- Confidence is not margin — follow the policy that exists to protect the margin.
- A short extension that is "very close to the dock" still has to be priced with the return, in the wind actually present.
- Report it even when you'd rather not. The report is the learning.
- Consider a higher landing target (15% → 20%) and keep the no-cancel-RTH rule firm, so the decision is made on the ground, not in the air.

### Case Study 2: Control interface froze twice on a long return → landed at ~10%
**Scenario:** On a mission about 8 km from the dock, the virtual cockpit and the main flight-control page both became unresponsive. Recovery required closing all tabs and opening a new tab to re-establish control. The transit home started at about 51% battery and the percentage dropped while this happened. About 400 m from the dock the interface froze again and the same fix was needed. By then the battery was about 15%. Winds gusted up to 12 m/s during the transit, and the parachute was disarmed for the return to prevent an accidental discharge.

**Outcome:** The aircraft landed with about 10% remaining. This was another pilot's incident, passed on as a learning example.

**Contributing factors / observations:**
- Two interface freezes, each costing time while the battery kept falling.
- Long range from the dock and strong, gusty wind on the return leg.
- Parachute disarmed for transit to avoid an accidental discharge, which also removes a layer of protection.

**Lessons:**
- Losing the interface is a battery event as much as a comms event — rehearse the recovery steps so they cost seconds, not minutes.
- Set the turn-back point with margin for a recovery delay, not only for the wind.
- A higher landing target (see Case Study 1) would have made this a less tight landing.

### Case Study 3: Missed trigger leaves a gap in delivered photo coverage
**Scenario:** After a wall-inspection flight, a client noticed a section of the route with no photos and asked why. Checking took about 15 minutes of pulling the data off the data platform and uploading ~250 images into FH2 to see where each was taken.

**Outcome:** All 242 images had been delivered with **no skipped capture numbers**. The gap was a **missed trigger** between two consecutive photos, with no warning in the flight record. An incident report was raised for follow-up. The Flight Photo Coverage Checker was built from this event.

**Lessons:**
- Start with image counts and capture numbers — it quickly separates a *delivery* problem from a *capture* problem.
- Equidistant capture can silently miss a trigger, and the flight report doesn't record capture events, so **the photos themselves are the best evidence**.
- Check coverage before delivering rather than waiting for the client to find the gap.

### Case Study 4: "Just like what you're cleared for" — a takeoff with no induction and no context
**Scenario:** A colleague who had not been inducted on a particular test procedure was told it was just like the visual flying they were already cleared for. They went out with a laptop, no backups and no clear picture of the procedure or the situation, to do it for the first time on that aircraft. The aircraft had just been calibrated. The pilot believed it would take off from the ground, but it was actually sitting on the hub, and they were told it was ready. After takeoff the aircraft turned toward a wall; the pilot stopped the input, but momentum carried it on and the propellers struck the wall and it fell. This was a second-hand account.

**Outcome:** The aircraft struck the wall and fell. Looking back, the right answer was to say no.

**Contributing factors / observations:**
- No induction for the procedure, and reassurance that it was "just like" something else.
- First time doing the procedure on this aircraft, with no backups and no clear picture of what was expected.
- An assumption about the launch point (ground vs hub) that nobody verified.
- "It's ready" from someone else substituted for the pilot's own situational awareness.

**Lessons:**
- Never assume. If you don't have the full context and aren't sure of the situation, don't do it.
- Check the launch point, the aircraft state and the clearance to the nearest obstacle yourself before takeoff, especially the first time you do a procedure.
- Being told it's fine is not the same as being briefed and inducted.
